An interface can look polished and still leave someone unsure what to do next. A dashboard may contain every number a team requested, yet make the most useful decision surprisingly hard to find. The challenge is often less about adding information and more about giving existing information a clear order.

## Start with a decision

Before choosing a layout, it helps to describe the task in an ordinary sentence. A person might need to check whether a delivery is delayed, decide which request needs attention, or confirm that a record was saved. Each task suggests a different hierarchy. The page should make the relevant state visible, explain what it means, and offer a clear next action.

That does not mean every screen needs one large button. Some screens are for comparison or exploration. Even there, a useful starting point matters: a sensible default filter, a meaningful heading, or a summary that explains what changed.

## Make feedback part of the design

A workflow continues after someone clicks. Loading, success, and error states deserve the same care as the first screen. Clear feedback helps people understand whether their action worked and what they can do if it did not. Keeping their input when something fails can matter more than a decorative animation.

## Leave room to learn

The first version is an opportunity to test an assumption. Watching where someone pauses can reveal more than asking whether a design looks good. A confusing label, an unexpected order, or a missing explanation is a useful signal to revise the interface.

This is a sample journal article for The Katalyst. Its central idea is simple: design around the next decision, then make that decision easier to understand.
